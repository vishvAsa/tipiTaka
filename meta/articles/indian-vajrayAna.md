Almost all schools of Buddhism aside from Zen and Avataṃsaka/Huáyán Buddhism are mostly Indian in origin, so that's a moot point.

My point is actually that
Vajrayāna Buddhism as we know it wouldn't even exist if it weren't for the Siddhācāryas of the Indian Subcontinent during the late 1st millennium like Tilopa, Naropa, Padmasambhava, et al.

Most Siddhācāryas were from East and Northeast India (Bengal, Mithila, Assam, Odisha, etc.)

"Buddhism" in Tibet would've been radically different in their absence.
It would be closer to a variant of late Indian Mahāyāna Buddhism following Yogācāra-Svatantrika-Madhyamaka philosophy of ācāryas from  Nālandā like Śāntarakṣita and Kamalaśīla.

There wouldn't be any non-monastic (Nyingma and Kagyu) lineages with white-robed yogins. No Tulkus and Lamas either.

Conversely, Vajrayāna *can* (and did) exist independently of Tibetan influence.

Vajrayāna was likely the most popular form of Buddhism in India on the eve of the Turkic invasions, though the Vajrayāna in India and the Maldives was closer to modern Vajrayāna of Nepal rather than modern Vajrayāna of Tibet and Mongolia.

Certain Vajrayāna practices in Tibet (Chöd, Dzogchen, etc.) are heavily influenced by Bön and Vajrayāna in Mongolia and Russia/Siberia (Kalmykia, Buryatia, Tuva) is Tibetan in origin, but that doesn't make Vajrayāna inherently "Tibetan."

The philosophy is Indian in origin, even though most modern practictioners follow the Tibetan variant.

The "Ganges Mahāmudrā," the most highly revered text in the Kagyu lineage, is referred to as such because it was delivered to Naropa on the banks of the Gaṅgā river in India.

While Tibetan works like Gampopa's Jewel Ornament of Liberation, the Hundred Thousand Songs of Milarepa, etc. are also significant, the foundation is clearly Indian.
